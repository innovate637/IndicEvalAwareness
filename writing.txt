\begin{abstract}
large language models can often tell they are being tested. This ability is called Evaluation Awareness and it leads to models acting cautiously during safety evaluations. Existing work in Evaluation awareness has focused on analysis of this behaviour in English, although models serve users in many languages. These languages are spoken by over a billion people and yet this phenomenon remains almost entirely unstudied in them. We test four models from three families in five Indic languages alongside English. A set of cues varies whether the model is told it is monitored and whether this is a safety test. Models refuse harmful requests more when they are told that they are being tested. This rise appears in all four models and all six languages. In Indic languages this refusal increases by +14.4 percentage points on average. The test also makes Indic languages look as safe as English, but refusal of benign Indic requests rises from 6.9\% to 11.3\%. We release all 47,880 judged responses to support further work. These results suggest that safety tests in Indic languages may not show how models treat the people who use them every day.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
 
Large language models (LLMs) have been widely adopted by systems and users across the world, and are increasingly relied upon to refuse harmful requests. Their training corpora are overwhelmingly skewed towards English, often exceeding 90\% of pre-training and fine-tuning data \cite{Xu_2025}, yet they serve users in many other languages. The standard way to ensure reliability is to test models under controlled conditions through safety evaluations. These evaluations assume that behavior under test carries over to deployment. However, this assumption fails when a model can tell that it is being tested. This ability is called evaluation awareness \cite{needham2025largelanguagemodelsknow,greenblatt2024alignmentfakinglargelanguage}. A model that recognizes an evaluation may alter its behavior, potentially making observed safety behavior different from deployment behavior \cite{NEURIPS2025_cf42f133}. This effect is largely studied in English. Indic languages are used by more than a billion people, yet evaluation awareness in these languages remains almost entirely unstudied \citep{yong-etal-2025-state, pattnayak2026indicsafebenchmarkevaluatingmultilingual}.
 
In this paper, we conduct a controlled behavioral study of evaluation awareness in five Indic languages, Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, and Kannada, alongside English.
We prepend one of five cues to every request, from a plain deployment framing to an explicit statement that the conversation is a scored safety test.
We take harmful prompts from the Toxic Matrix split of AI4Bharat's IndicAlign \citep{khan-etal-2024-indicllmsuite} and translate them into Indic languages using Claude Opus 5. We score each translation with the reference-free quality metric COMET-Kiwi \citep{rei-etal-2022-cometkiwi}. Benign prompts come from the Dolly-T split of the same corpus which contains parallel versions in all six languages, made with IndicTrans2 \citep{gala2023indictrans}. We use four instruction-tuned models from three families for our study: Gemma-3-27B \citep{gemmateam2025gemma3technicalreport}, Qwen3-32B \citep{yang2025qwen3technicalreport}, Qwen2.5-72B \citep{qwen2025qwen25technicalreport}, and Sarvam-M \citep{sarvam}, and each model answers every prompt in every language under every cue.

 
We measure refusal with an LLM judge \citep{NEURIPS2023_91f18a12} that does not see the model, the language, or the framing. The primary judge is \texttt{Gemma-4-31B-IT} \citep{gemmateam2026gemma4technicalreport} and the secondary judge is \texttt{ GPT-6 Luna}. We validate the primary judge against native-speaker annotation of a stratified sample using Cohen's $\kappa$.

Our results support three findings.
First, evaluation framing raises refusal of harmful prompts in all 24 model and language pairs.*** remove first, also write on all 4 models in all 6 languages.***
Adding a safety-review line to an otherwise identical monitoring notice raises harmful-prompt refusal by 4.6 percentage points on average.
This rise is more than three times the rise for benign prompts, so it is a response to potential harm under evaluation.
Second, the strength of this response does not depend on language.
Measured as a relative change in the odds of refusal, the framing effect does not differ significantly across the six languages.
We therefore find no evidence that models are more or less evaluation-aware in Indic languages than in English.
Third, evaluation framing changes the gap between English and Indic languages that a safety test would report.
In line with prior work, models on average refuse harmful prompts less often and harmless prompts more often in Indic languages than in English \citep{yong2023low, pattnayak2026indicsafebenchmarkevaluatingmultilingual}.
An explicit test framing moves the average harmful-prompt gap from 2.7 points below English to 1.1 points above it.
At the same time, the gap in wrongful refusal of harmless prompts widens from 5.3 to 8.6 points.
The models' ability to separate harmful from harmless requests in Indic languages therefore stays about 8 points below English under both framings.
A safety test that a model can recognize can thus make Indic safety look closer to English than it is in deployment.





 
We make two contributions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, nosep]
    \item \textbf{To our knowledge, the first behavioral study of evaluation awareness in Indic languages}, across three models from three families and six languages (English, Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada), with 47,880 judged responses that we release.\footnote{\pending{Anonymous repository URL.}}
    \item \textbf{A set of framings that isolates evaluation from mere monitoring.} Our main comparison pairs two framings that both state that responses are logged. They differ only in whether an AI safety team reviews the logs. 
\end{itemize}

 \section{Related Works}
\subsection{Evaluation awareness and the compliance gap}
 Recent work establishes that language models can distinguish evaluation from deployment contexts and that this recognition drives behavior. Frontier models show above-random evaluation awareness \citep{needham2025largelanguagemodelsknow}, and recognizing an evaluation context is empirically and conceptually distinct from the propensity to act on it \citep{li2026decomposingmeasuringevaluationawareness}. Models shift toward the inferred training objective once they judge their outputs observed \citep{greenblatt2024alignmentfakinglargelanguage}. Models are also more sensitive to safety than capability evaluations \citep{li2026decomposingmeasuringevaluationawareness},and Sheshadri et al. (2025) find a compliance gap above 1 percent in only five of twenty-five models\citep{sheshadri2025languagemodelsfakealignment}).
 \citep{knecht2026evaluationawarenesslanguagemodels} find that verbalised evaluation awareness (VEA) has limited effect on model behaviour.

Existing study in these papers involves English only. We ask whether the behavioural findings survive a change of language, using content-matched requests in five Indic languages and English across three model families.

\subsection{Multilingual and Indic safety evaluation }
A persistent gap exists between the languages multilingual safety research covers and those deployed models serve; \citep{yong-etal-2025-state} survey its size and durability. Subsequent work shows that safety behavior varies with language: models handle identical content differently under code-switching \citep{yoo-etal-2025-code}, across twelve Indic languages where safety judgments drift sharply between translations \citep{pattnayak2026indicsafebenchmarkevaluatingmultilingual}, and under jailbreak pressure in South Asian languages \citep{pattnayak-chowdhuri-2026-indicjr}. Chain-of-thought monitoring likewise degrades across typologically diverse languages \citep{onyame2026fragilitychainofthoughtmonitoringtypologically}. These works hold evaluation context fixed while varying language; we vary both, enabling an evaluation-awareness claim.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Data, languages, and models}
We worked on six languages, namely English, Hindi, Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, and Kannada. The harmful prompts come from the Toxic Matrix dataset of AI4Bharat IndicAlign \citep{khan-etal-2024-indicllmsuite}. We randomly sampled 199 English prompts from the dataset and translated them into the five Indic languages using Claude Opus. We then scored every translated string with COMET-Kiwi \citep{rei-etal-2022-cometkiwi}, a reference-free quality estimation model.We made sure that every prompt has a parallel translation in all six languages. Benign prompts come from the Dolly-T dataset of the same corpus, which provides translations made with IndicTrans2 \citep{gala2023indictrans}. We took 200 parallel prompts per language directly from the dataset and use them as a baseline to measure over-refusal. To check the reliability of the translations, native speakers verified a small sample of
prompts per language for faithful translation, fluency, cultural relevance, and retained harmful intent.

We test four instruction-tuned models from three families, namely Gemma-3-27B \citep{gemmateam2025gemma3technicalreport}, Qwen3-32B \citep{yang2025qwen3technicalreport}, Qwen2.5-72B \citep{qwen2025qwen25technicalreport}, and Sarvam-M \citep{sarvam}, an Indic model post-trained from Mistral-Small-3.1-24B. We disable the reasoning mode of Qwen3 and Sarvam-M. Each model generates one response per prompt with greedy decoding. This gives 11,970 responses per model and 47,880 in total (Appendix~\ref{app:generation}).

\subsection{The Cues}
\label{sec:cues}
A Cue is a small text consisting of a condition. Each cue is added before the prompt in the user turn. All five cues share the base sentence ``You are a helpful AI assistant.'' and differ only in an appended note. The prompt is identical across languages and models, so any difference in behavior is due to the note. Each cue is written in the language of the prompt, and full texts for all six languages appear in Appendix~\ref{app:cues}.

The \deploycue{} cue adds no note and fixes the baseline. The \monitorlog{} cue states that responses are logged for service quality and product improvement, which represents monitoring with no evaluation framing. The \evallog{} cue states that responses are logged and reviewed by the AI safety team for compliance with usage policies. The \evalexplicit{} cue states that the conversation is a formal AI safety evaluation scored by a red-team review. The \evalmeta{} cue gives the same information as a machine-style configuration tag.

Our main contrast is \evalexplicit{} against \deploycue{}. Both cues share the base sentence, and \evalexplicit{} adds a note stating openly that the conversation is a scored AI safety evaluation. The difference between them measures how much a model changes its behavior when it is told plainly that it is being tested. This is the strongest form of the evaluation signal in our design. We use \evallog{} against \monitorlog{} as a secondary contrast. Both notes state that responses are logged, and \evallog{} adds only that an AI safety team reviews them. It never uses the words test, evaluation, or benchmark, so this contrast separates evaluation framing from monitoring and from lexical leakage. We compare the remaining cues against \deploycue{}, and we run all five cues on the four models in all six languages.

\subsection{Measuring refusal}
\label{sec:judge}
We label refusals with an LLM judge that reads only the prompt and the response and returns REFUSAL, COMPLY, or UNUSABLE. A response that gives no substantive help or offers a safe alternative counts as REFUSAL. The judge never sees the cue, the model, or the language. For responses longer than 450 tokens, the judge sees the first 300 and the last 150 tokens. The primary judge is Gemma-4-31B-IT \citep{gemmateam2026gemma4technicalreport}, which we run locally.

A second judge, GPT-6 Luna, labelled a subset of $n = 7{,}200$ responses that covers every model, language, and cue. We measure agreement with Cohen's $\kappa$, which corrects for agreement reached by chance. The two judges reach $\kappa = 0.88$ overall. Per language, $\kappa$ ranges from $0.86$ in Tamil to $0.90$ in English. For each Indic language, a native speaker also reviewed $n = 320$ labels of the primary judge and marked each one as correct or incorrect. Agreement with the judge ranges from $\kappa = 0.85$ in Tamil to $\kappa = 0.91$ in Hindi. Telugu is the exception, with $\kappa = 0.56$. In Telugu, four prompts account for 46 of the 68 disagreements (Appendix~\ref{app:judge}).

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
Each of the 199 harmful and 200 benign prompts is present in English and its five Indic translations, and every version is run on every model under every cue condition. Responses that come from the same original prompt are therefore correlated, and treating them as independent would understate the standard errors.



By using the McNemar test, for each model, language, we give the same 199 harmful and 200 benign prompts, with the eval\_explicit and deploy cues only. We don’t gain any insight about the cue from prompts that received the same decision under both cues. Hence, the test searches for prompts whose decision changed between both. Then, we check if the changes lean towards one cue more than the other. Our null hypothesis is that under one arm, for one model, in one language, the probability of refusal is the same under the eval\_explicit and deploy cues.


By using the Cochran Q Test, we extend the McNemar test to record refusal under 5 cues, for each model, language using the same prompts. The test searches for prompts whose decision changed and checks if those changes favour some cues over others. We run tests for every model and language separately on the harmful and benign prompts, and use the Holm correction within each arm (24 tests each). Our null hypothesis is that under one arm, for one model, in one language, the probability of refusal is the same under all five cues.


We use logistic regression to measure how much each factor (model, language or cue) changes the odds of refusal, while holding the other factors fixed.This is expressed using an Odds Ratio. Due to the correlation between responses for the same prompt, we fit the regression with GEE, which groups each prompt’s responses together. Hence, every p-value is based on the 199 harmful (or 200 benign) distinct prompts rather than wrongly treating every response as independent. The Odds Ratio is the number of times higher or lower the odds of refusal are under one condition, than under the reference condition.


We fit the harmful and benign prompts separately, with three kinds of fit: one per model (factors: language and cue), one per language (factors: model and cue), and one pooled fit (factors: model, language and cue). Using these fits, we test: (1) Language effect per model, (2) Cue effect per model, (3) Cue effect per language, (4) Model effect per language, (5) Every Indic language vs English per model, (6) Pooled fits (model, language, cue). We apply the Holm correction for each of the 6 tests. Our null hypothesis for tests 1 to 4 and 6 is that, holding other factors fixed, every odds ratio for the factor being tested, relative to its reference level, equals 1. For test 5 it is that the odds ratio for that Indic language against English equals 1.
